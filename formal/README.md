# formal: Words, Their Indexes, and Their Alignment over Semirings

*Status: draft (2026-10-24): the first slice, words and their indexes, semirings, the alignment of two words in any semiring, and the polytope algebra ([decision 0005](../development/decisions/0005-words-their-indexes-and-their-alignment.md)); the run-length FM-index, the r-index and the positional BWT; grammars, their parsers over semirings, and grammars with symmetry (decision 0006).* Automata beyond Aho–Corasick, transducers, rewriting systems, transition systems and bimorphisms are planned (morphe's README, §2).

## 1. Purpose

Formal structures: words and the structures built on them. This first slice serves the sequences of the sciences, the genomes of The Science Infrastructures' genomics domain first, and the texts of its linguistics next: the combinatorics of words, the indexes that make a long word searchable, and the alignment of two words, defined once as a sum over the paths of an edit graph in a semiring.

## 2. Concepts

- **Words and their morphisms.** A word is an element of the free monoid A* on an alphabet A = [0, σ); a morphism of monoids is fixed by the images of the letters (a substitution), and iterated it generates the classical infinite words, Thue–Morse, Fibonacci, period-doubling (Lothaire 1983, 2002). Borders and periods (Fine and Wilf), primitive roots (Lyndon and Schützenberger), conjugacy and least rotations are computed exactly, with the algorithms of Knuth, Morris and Pratt, Gusfield's Z-array and Booth.
- **Lyndon words.** A word strictly smaller than all its proper rotations; every word factors uniquely into a non-increasing product of Lyndon words (Chen, Fox and Lyndon 1958), found in linear time by Duval's algorithm, and their number is Witt's necklace polynomial, checked exactly against enumeration.
- **Suffix arrays and the Burrows–Wheeler transform.** The suffixes of a word sorted, by SA-IS in linear time (Nong, Zhang and Chan 2011); the longest common prefixes of neighbours (Kasai et al. 2001); the BWT, the last column of the sorted rotations, a permutation that groups equal letters and is inverted by the LF map. The bijective BWT (Gil and Scott, Kufleitner) needs no sentinel: it is the extended BWT of the word's Lyndon factors, a composite of three bijections, and the Gessel–Reutenauer correspondence reads multisets of necklaces off any word.
- **Indexes.** Bit vectors with rank in constant time and select; wavelet matrices; the FM-index, whose backward search counts the occurrences of a pattern in time proportional to its length (Ferragina and Manzini 2000); the Aho–Corasick automaton of a dictionary.
- **Run-length indexes.** A word made of many copies of one text with a few differences has a BWT of few runs, r ≪ n. The run-length FM-index keeps the BWT as its runs and counts a pattern by backward search with rank by run heads and lengths (Mäkinen and Navarro 2005); the r-index locates its occurrences in O(r) words, from samples of the suffix array at the boundaries of runs: the toehold gives the suffix-array value of the last row of the pattern's interval, and φ, SA[i] ↦ SA[i − 1], every other one (Gagie, Navarro and Prezza 2018, 2020). For a power w^k of a primitive word of length p, r ≤ 2p + 1 whatever k (proved in `r_index.hpp`).
- **The positional BWT.** For M words of one length, the prefix arrays a_k sort them at each position k by their prefixes read backwards, and the divergence arrays d_k give where each word's common factor with its predecessor, ending at k, begins; a_{k+1} is a stable partition of a_k, so both are built column by column in O(MNσ). From them, every long match (length ≥ L) and every set-maximal match of the panel, and of a new word against it, the basis of haplotype copying (Durbin 2014; Li and Stephens 2003).
- **Semirings and the edit graph.** An alignment of two words is a path in their edit graph; a scoring scheme assigns its steps weights in a semiring S, and the sum over paths of the products of weights is, in (max, +), the optimal score; in ℕ, the number of alignments (the Delannoy numbers for global ones); in (max, +) with multiplicities, the optimum and the number of optimal alignments; in the polytope algebra, the Newton polygon whose vertices are every alignment optimal for some choice of parameters (Pachter and Sturmfels 2004). Semiring homomorphisms carry one reading to another: forgetting multiplicities, or evaluating a polygon's support function at a parameter, which is the tropical value there.
- **Grammars.** Phrase-structure grammars, the context-free and the monotone (context-sensitive) among them (Chomsky 1959; Hopcroft and Ullman 1979). Earley's parser decides every context-free grammar, ε-rules included by Aycock and Horspool's treatment of nullables, and reads a parse tree back. A weighted grammar in a semiring has inside and outside values on every span (Goodman 1999): recognition, the number of parse trees, the Viterbi parse, and, over the reals, the expected number of uses of every rule, the E-step of the inside–outside algorithm (Lari and Young 1990). A monotone grammar's membership is decided by searching its forms of bounded length.
- **Grammars with symmetry.** A finite group acting on a grammar's letters, by automorphisms of words or, through a sign, by anti-automorphisms (reversal), and fixing its start. An invariant grammar's language is invariant; its rules fall into Burnside's number of orbits, at least |R|/|G|. The equivariant Earley parser stores one rule per orbit and binds each rule's group element as a coset, narrowed at each scan: it decides exactly the language, its chart the plain chart taken up to the action. Matching terminals by their orbits alone decides the larger π⁻¹(L(Γ/G)). Spans of one class share their nonterminals' values; the canonical forms of a monotone grammar's search visit the orbits of the derivable forms. These make exact the group-equivariant grammars of Huang's draft (2026), whose amendments the genomics domain's note `theory/group-grammars.md` sets out.
- **Breaking.** Letter-to-letter maps of alphabets: the image of a context-free grammar generates the image of its language; the breakings of a torsor at its base points differ by right translations; a coarse alphabet's inside–outside pass masks the fine pass's spans exactly, keeping every complete parse.
- **The polytope algebra.** Lattice polygons, with ⊕ the convex hull of a union and ⊗ the Minkowski sum, computed exactly in integer arithmetic; the normal fan of the alignment polygon divides the space of parameters into the cones where each vertex is optimal (Gusfield, Balasubramanian and Naor 1994; Waterman, Eggert and Lander 1992).

## 7. Open Questions

1. **Three-dimensional polytopes**, for parametric alignment with affine gaps or in local mode.
2. **Automata** beyond Aho–Corasick, and grammars beyond the context-free and monotone ones written, for linguistics: deterministic and weighted automata (whose shortest-distance problems are the same semiring sums), context-free grammars and their parsing.
3. **Large indexes.** The FM-index is in memory with 32- or 64-bit indices; external-memory construction would carry it to whole human genomes. The r-index is built from the full suffix array, so its construction needs O(n) words although the index keeps O(r); building it in space near r (by prefix-free parsing, for instance) would carry it to large collections of genomes. The positional BWT keeps every a_k and d_k, (N + 1)M words each; Durbin's run-length coding of the columns would make it small.
